# Core Concept
- Hedging is like buying an insurance, providing protection for uncertainty and limiting downside risk. 
- While hedging, profits cannot be maximized, but hedging is not about making profits, it's about limiting loss.
- Hedging is to manage risk by offsetting potential losses in an investment
- The way to do so is by taking an opposing position in a related asset to minimize exposure to adverse price movement
- Not only downside risk can be hedged, volatility can also be hedged
- Common ways to hedge is using derivatives and diversification

# Pros
- Hedging allows the investors to be protected from large loss, especially in volatile markets
- By reducing downside risk, hedging allows investors to maintain more predictable returns and financial performances
- Hedging also proivdes flexibility in different market conditions, so investors can tailor different strategies base on market movement

# Tradeoffs (not cons)
- Hedging requires investor to pay for the protection, which is called the premium. Even if the market did goes as what expected, that means the hedge is not initially needed, the premium still has to be paid, which hence limits the potential profit.
- Hedging is less precise compared to insurance although they are similar to each another. Insurance guarantees the compensation but hedging can easily go wrong, which requires expertise to execute the hedging strategy.
# Quick Comparison
- Rick buys **200 shares of Galactic Corp** at **$50 per share**, investing a total of **$10,000**. 
- To hedge against potential downside risk, he purchases a **put option with a strike price of $45** that expires in one year. 
- The option costs **$2 per share**, so he pays a total premium of **$400** (200 shares × $2).
- **Scenario 1: Stock Rises to $60**
	- If **Galactic Corp** is trading at **$60** per share after a year, Rick will **not** exercise the put option since the market price is higher. His put option expires worthless, and he loses the **$400 premium** he paid. However, his unrealized profit is **$2,000** (\[$60 - $50\] × 200 shares), making the hedge a small cost for protection. And he's still making profit at $1600.
- **Scenario 2: Stock Drops to $30**
	- If the stock price falls to **$30**, Rick will exercise the put option and sell his shares for **$45** each instead of the market price of $30. His total proceeds from the sale will be **$9,000** (200 shares × $45), resulting in a loss of **$1,400** (\[$10,000 initial investment - $9,000 from the sale\] + $400 premium cost).
- Without the put option, Rick would have lost **$4,000** (\[$10,000 - $6,000\]). The hedge significantly reduces his downside risk, limiting his loss to **$1,400 instead of $4,000**.
- While hedging costs money, it helps investors like Rick manage risk and protect their investments from extreme downturns.

# Why don't I buy less at first place?
- The upside potential is limited when buying less at first place. To replicate the limited loss scenario at $1,400, Rick would buy 70 shares instead of 200 shares at first place, when the price falls to $30, he would loss $1,400, however, when the share do goes up to $60, he would only earn $700. Whereas buying the option would protect the loss same at $1400, but the earnings would be $1600.
- Investment is not only focusing on one asset but the overall portfolio allocation. Investing less in one asset will disrupt the balance, where hedging allows investors to maintain the planned allocation and protect against downside risk.
